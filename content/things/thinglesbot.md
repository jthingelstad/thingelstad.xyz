Thinglesbot was Jamie’s robot, and it lived on Twitter. He had scripts and tools like cacti watching things around the house, but after a while nobody looks at that output, so he gave them a voice. A separate account, @thinglesBot, posted the outdoor temperature four times a day along with readings from the attic and the utility room, said when the solar panels turned on and off, and reported how many emails were in his inbox at the end of each day. It was “nothing more than a collection of scripts” on a few computers, posting with curl. He [introduced it](https://www.thingelstad.com/2007/03/11/introducing-my-robot.html) on March 11, 2007, and two people he had never met, in other states, soon followed it.

When he quit Twitter in November 2007 he wrote that [the robot’s account had been deleted](https://www.thingelstad.com/2007/11/04/buhbye-twitter.html), but Thinglesbot was [posting temperatures again](https://web.archive.org/web/20080218023438/http://twitter.com/thinglesbot/statuses/696031342) by February 2008. After it lost its sensor connections and went quiet, he [rewired it](https://www.thingelstad.com/2009/03/24/my-robot-returns.html) on March 24, 2009, replacing the Bash and curl scripts with Ruby and the Twitter4R library and adding a daily favorite number, color, or letter and a “favorite follower.” Its posts were also collected on [thinglesbot.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20110209234653/http://thinglesbot.com/) (“I am Jamie’s robot”).

In February 2012 he listed thinglesBot among the sites he [shut down](https://www.thingelstad.com/2012/02/08/shutting-down-some.html), and in 2014 among his deleted Twitter accounts ([Twitter Spam #NFL](https://www.thingelstad.com/2014/11/28/twitter-spam-nfl.html)). It was the first of his agents.
