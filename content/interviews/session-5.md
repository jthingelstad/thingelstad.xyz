### 31. In Weekly Thing 354 you wrote, "A world that doesn't need new software is a world without ideas." How has agentic coding changed what you build and how fast?

**Jamie:** Oh, I mean, night and day. I have said many times that agentic coding and AI in general makes your impossible list possible. And that couldn't be more true for me and what I've been doing. I would say that, I mean, if you look at my GitHub activity for every year that I've had an account, it's almost nothing. And then this year it explodes. And that's entirely because of agents. The ability to build and create solutions that far exceed my skills to actually be able to assemble them has been kind of intoxicating, frankly. Almost too much, like a rabbit hole that you dive down and maybe, you know, you fall way, way down in it. So I would say the speed and ability to create these things has been a huge unlock. And frankly, none of this stuff would exist if that weren't the case. They would all be on my list of impossible projects.

### 32. What do these things cost you, in money and in hours, and how do you think about that?

**Jamie:** Yeah, in money, now with AI, I obviously have subscriptions to multiple AI services, ChatGPT and Claude, and those add up. I also have costs that are incurred in AWS to host all the various projects that I am building and running. In terms of time, there's kind of a continuous amount of time that I put into publishing in the Weekly Thing. And then I have kind of project time on top of that. But both those costs and the time, fundamentally, I would connect back to a core thesis I have around learning. I believe strongly that to have a career in technology is to have a career in learning. And what I do when I'm building is I'm learning. And it's really an investment in my craft and an investment in the art of creating. That then impacts so many other things around me. I can tell you, for example, that I'm better at being a CTO because I have direct hands-on experience creating things and I know the trade-offs and kind of the art of the possible in a way that just isn't possible if all you do is read and kind of watch others. It allows me to connect with my team and understand what transformation actually feels like not just what the business results are of it. And frankly, it's just what I'm passionate about. I love doing it. And so I feel like this feels very much more like art to me than hustles. It's not a way to create a business. It's a way to create something of beauty and something that I see in my head that I want to instantiate in the real world.

### 33. Is there a thing you've wanted to build for years and haven't?

**Jamie:** Honestly, I'm kind of doing that right now. I mean, Road Sign Math getting created in the last few days was one of the last things on my list. So I've worked through my list and created most of the things that are on that list. Now I've been going and doing kind of a V2 on some of those. If you look at what I did with Linking Things recently to make it this 3D spatial kind of flyover, that's a way of looking at that. So I am 100% sure that new ideas will come. Actually, just recently I had the idea that Thingy should actually be a 3D model and be able to move around and engage with people. So how do you do that? Like I said, a world that doesn't need software is a world without ideas. I've got plenty of ideas.

### 34. When you look at the whole registry at once, what pattern do you see that you didn't notice while building?

**Jamie:** I mean, it made the threads and eras more visible. I know that I had a MediaWiki era or a wiki era in general. I knew that I had a crypto era in general. And I knew that the blog was the backbone of all of it, and it made that a lot more obvious. There were a handful of projects like VeloTweets that I had totally forgotten about, and it was delightful to see that come back to life. And I absolutely adore the idea that Things I've built has these artifacts where I can capture artifacts that are part of the building process. It may not even be part of the actual final website. I honestly feel like that will give a view, kind of like my blog is an artifact that I can read in the future. I think the Things I've built site will be a way of capturing and remembering those things in a much deeper way.

### 35. Twenty years from now, what do you hope is still running?

**Jamie:** For sure, my blog. For sure, the Weekly Thing. And I hope at that point, Another Thing has a vibrant and growing published history of new podcasts. The Thingy agent, for sure, getting increasingly more intelligent and capable to interface with everything that I've created over the years. I'm sure 20 years from now, Elixir will be done and retired. I think it's unlikely that I'd be playing that game in 20 years. I think the museum would still be there. I think that I'll have more archival sites, so like the Proof of a Person archive and the nnxyz museum are kind of a new class of thing, something where it's an activity I did, and I kind of created that as a capstone that will just exist in perpetuity. Almost a time capsule. I would expect that there will be more of those things because that's kind of a new lever, a new kind of thing that I can do with AI that I was never able to do before. And I'm sure that there will be some new stuff that has not even come into my mind yet.
