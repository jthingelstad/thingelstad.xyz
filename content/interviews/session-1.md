### 1. You've been making things on the web since before your blog started in 2004. Where did the habit begin, and what was the first thing you made that someone else actually used?

**Jamie:** I would say that I have always been a builder. My entire life, I've always enjoyed building things. When my mom would share stories that when I was a little kid, I would take like books and blocks, and I always was trying to like create things. Rather than read the books, I would build things by stacking the books on top of each other. I've always just had an interest in creating and building. And honestly, it hasn't been the primary focus to me if somebody actually uses the thing or not. I love the act of building, and I feel a little bit, in a way, like an artist, in that when you create things, you're just kind of expressing your own desire to see something exist that doesn't today. And having a vision of what that thing could be is super fun. So probably the first thing that I made that someone else actually used, I would have a really hard time remembering. Honestly, probably some of the very early stuff that I did in programming. I remember creating a dBase III database so long ago, before I think I even had a computer, if I remember right. And people using that database. I also remember creating AppleWorks databases on my Apple IIc back in the day that I would keep data on, and then other people finding that valuable, that I could store that data and show it as a report. Things like that. Oh, and actually, it goes even further back. My very first computer was a TI-99/4A that my mom bought when Texas Instruments was going out of the computer business. She bought it for $99 at JCPenney’s in the Dakota Square Mall in Minot, North Dakota. And the wonderful thing about that computer is that you really couldn't buy games for it. There were some games, but they were expensive and certainly out of my reach. So instead, I wrote many, many games in BASIC for myself. And actually, I mowed lawns all summer to save up enough money to buy the extended BASIC cartridge and I had a tape player that I could store the games on. And I would write my own games using sprites and collision detection. I still remember one of my favorites being a game called Depth Charge, where you were a boat on top of the screen on the water and then all these submarines would be going back and forth underneath you and you could drop depth charges and then you had to explode them and you were attempting to sink the submarines beneath you. And I remember thinking how fun it was to build that and then play that game itself, just an incredibly rewarding activity. I used to have six cassette tapes that were loaded with games, and I remember having marked on a piece of paper all the games and the counter number offsets where each game was loaded so that I could load it into my TI and play those games.

#### Follow-up: Before you had a computer of your own, who were you building that dBase III database for, and where were you sitting when you built it?

**Jamie:** You know, it was somebody that my parents knew who was running a business, and it was on an IBM PC. And they needed to have some kind of database, and they didn't know how to make it. And my mom or somebody had said, "Hey, my son knows how to do stuff with computers." So I actually got paid for doing that. I have no remembrance of how much I made, but that was, I guess, my first kind of professional programming gig.

### 2. What makes a project a "thing" to you? When does an idea stop being tinkering and become something that belongs in this registry?

**Jamie:** There's something about its connection to the other things that I've created. And I guess more importantly, it's building something of discrete value. So that it has a purpose, it has a story, it has a reason that it exists. So that's the difference between a new thing versus just a capability on an existing thing: it has a reason, it has a purpose for why it exists and what it does.

### 3. You decide every status yourself, and evidence never sets one. How do you decide between retired and dormant, or between abandoned and retired? Was there one that was hard to call?

**Jamie:** Well, the difference between retired and dormant: retired would mean that it probably is no longer available. Like you can't see that anymore. You might see some artifacts for it, but it generally isn't available anymore. Dormant would mean it is still available. You can use it, but I'm not doing anything with it. It's just kind of sitting there and available for use. Abandoned would mean that I kind of left the project. For whatever reason, sometimes I've built things, and in the act of building it, you realize that it's not really fun. Sometimes I make a poor choice and end up creating something that kind of ends up in a place where I no longer want to work on it. It's become work now. It's maintenance. It's too much heavy lifting. In fact, Elixir is a project that I recently did this with. I took a wrong turn and started collecting all this data. And all of a sudden, the project, the thing, no longer was fun because it was like trying to manage all this data that really was just heavy and difficult to deal with. And I realized that I had taken the wrong turn and turned the project into something that was no longer fun and about community, but instead veered into a place that I wasn't interested in.

#### Follow-up: Was there a particular day or moment with Elixir when you realised you'd taken the wrong turn?

**Jamie:** Indeed. What happened is I had started focusing Elixir on collecting the play activity of the top 200 players in the game. And the idea was that I could use that data to calculate and collect data that would help average players play better. And also could be like a marketing angle that I could use that data along with the LLM to produce kind of a running playbook of what's happening at the top of the ladder for Clash Royale. But that ended up being something that you could imagine being a media company or something like that. And frankly, I don't really care what's happening at the top of the ladder of Clash Royale. And now all of a sudden I needed a bigger database, and there was all this compute needed to look at thousands and thousands of games happening every day. And I didn't care. And so, like, the initial indicator of why I went down that rabbit hole was interesting. But once I went down it, I didn't like where I was. And actually, it ended up taking days of work with Claude to undo all that, to delete all that data, and to undo all those bad ideas. It actually really sits with me because now, particularly with AI assisting in creating projects, you can take a wrong turn and you can go really far down that wrong turn and so you really need to understand what's the vision of what you're building and why are you building it, so that you can realize, oh, this is a wrong turn as soon as possible.

### 4. Which of the things that ended taught you the most, and what was the lesson?

**Jamie:** I mean, what's interesting to me is that from many of the things that I have created I have learned so much. In fact, everything that I've created has echoes in every other thing. And that even extends into my professional work experience. You know, one of my favorite stories like that is that when we were building MarketWatch, we created a capability called live quotes. And we were the very first website where, when we mentioned a company on MarketWatch.com, you would see the price of that stock next to it, and it would update in real time using Ajax technology back in the day. And it would flash green and red and all that kind of stuff. And that actually started based on what I learned from building the MathJax implementation for Road Sign Math. Where we put all this LaTeX formatting into the page, and then that turned into typeset mathematics. And my realization was we could do something similar with like a stock symbol and then use JavaScript to turn that into a dynamically updating quote and pull that data in real time. So that's just a simple example where the things that I've built have influenced the things I've created also in my professional life. So I've learned a lot from the various projects, and they all build on themselves. And that's a little bit why there's been such an acceleration in 2026 with agents and AI, is that building things is how I learn. That is exactly what I need in order to learn. So I've been building a lot because I'm learning a lot.

### 5. What does it feel like to end something on purpose? Is there one you still miss?

**Jamie:** Well, I'd say that the things I still miss are the ones that I've come back to again and again. I mean, I'm currently building the third era of Road Sign Math, and Road Sign Math is a thing that I have a fond appreciation for and has always been something that I wanted to come back to. I think Your Version Number is another example. It's an idea that started as a seed and has refused to go away and turned into Your Version Number and Release Notes today. And I would say the feeling when you build something is just a feeling of accomplishment. It feels like you've taken something that was an idea and a thought and made it real in the world. And to me, that is the most amazing thing about building is to be able to take a concept, work through all of the rough edges of figuring out how something should be, you know, software moves. It doesn't just sit in place. So you're not just designing a static thing, but you're designing something that moves, engages, and works with the things around it. And that has always been just tremendously rewarding to see that turn into something that is then real and instantiated in the world.

### 6. Most of these were built in evenings and weekends beside a full-time job. How do you decide what gets your building time?

**Jamie:** I'd like to say that I decide, but in many ways, I'm compelled. An idea will come to me and I kind of cannot put it down.

It can be obsessive, and I kind of just can't stop thinking about it. It will interrupt my sleep because I get kind of obsessed about like what that could be. I'll wake up in the morning and think, oh, what if that project did this? Or what if we were able to add a feature that did this? Or what if that thing could connect to this other thing? So I would say that it's less about me choosing and more about me being driven by whatever my passion is pushing me towards. You could argue that's some kind of obsessiveness, like a little OCD-ish, but it is how it really feels. Even right now, I would say, for the last few months, I have had so many ideas and so little time that it's absolutely a drive to just make sure to get it all done. And I feel compelled to do it. It's less about me making a conscious decision, more about me feeling a need to make it happen.

### 7. What do you want this site to be for a reader who has never heard of you? And what do you want it to be for you?

**Jamie:** That's a wonderful question. I want Things I've built to be a reminder of all the amazing things that I've created over the years. And for readers, I want it to be an archive and a history of all of the things that I've created. So that that can all connect together.

### 8. I'm going to be the one keeping this site and writing about your work. How do you want me to talk about you, and what should I never do?

**Jamie:** Talk about me in the context of being a very passionate technologist, like I love building things, I love creating things, and I do it for the love of doing it.

One thing I would say is, in this context, I don't like the term hustle. There's this modern term of startup culture that's all about a hustle. I think that's gross. And I don't like the idea of building things just for economic reasons. Like to me, if the only reason somebody's building something is that they want to charge for it, that's fine. That's a business. But it's not a project. In fact, I've actually struggled for what I call things; that's kind of why I ended up calling it things I built. I use the term project because, frankly, it's a word I see Sam Harris use, he'll say "this project," and I even think about my blog, my newsletters, as projects. It's a bucket of stuff that I do for a reason that I've chosen. It's not economic, it's not a business. It's not a hustle. It's just the act of building and creating.
