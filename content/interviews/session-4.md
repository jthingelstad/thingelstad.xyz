### 26. VeloTweets, Thingelstad Photography, Foreground, Filament, Flight Path, Numbers2Words, ParseURL. Pick the ones you remember with feeling. What were they for?

**Jamie:** You know, I mean, most of those actually I don't have a fondness for that much. What I would say is, VeloTweets made me smile a lot because I had kind of forgotten about that project. But it really reminded me of the best of times for Twitter. When there were tons of professional cyclists using it, Twitter was just fun. It hadn't become the kind of gross platform that I think it is now. And it was a great, fun project that I built with a couple of friends. It had a good run, and it made tons of sense as just aggregating things around a collection of users. Stuff that actually just predated what a lot of Twitter clients ended up doing with lists and things like that. We essentially were the precursor of much of that. Foreground and Filament are fun because it reminds me of the MediaWiki era and really focusing on building something compelling around that. I think it is cool that Foreground was eventually adopted by the Wikimedia collection. You know, it's now managed by Wikimedia. And it's become a supported project of that ecosystem. A number of MediaWiki sites use Foreground. And I will always remember Flight Path because it was one of my first agentic projects, like vibe coding, if you will. Pulling the POAP ecosystem together and bringing it into a 3D visualization of activity around the globe. And it really was a wow for me on what I was able to do and how AI could help me build solutions in ways that I would have never been able to do. I mean, Flight Path combined a lot of stuff that was not that hard to connect together, but really difficult for me. And I was able to do it literally via prompt on my phone and have a solution up and running in a couple of hours. It was incredible.

### 27. TOSRedux and the Weekly Thing Workshop are the two you call abandoned. What did you try, and when did you know?

**Jamie:** Yeah, TOSRedux, that project really is about embracing the privacy values that I have. And the idea that I had was we should be able to crowdsource amongst a lot of people what terms of service actually mean, what the privacy policies actually mean, and if you could then combine that with a browser extension that essentially put in plain English what the website that you were about to engage in was asking to do with your data, that would help people make a more informed decision about whether they want to be part of this platform. My belief is that many people have no idea what the terms of services are saying and what they actually mean. You know, doing that on a wiki was interesting, but there's just friction all over the place, and ultimately you have this problem of: does anybody really care? I have become fairly cynical that while I believe privacy is really important, I don't think your average user really gives it much value, and I don't think it would have gotten much adoption. And today I would look at that and just say, well, this is easily done with AI. But I think you're kind of swimming upstream with a general public that says, you know, that they don't think anything is private anyways, and they've kind of given up, frankly. I refuse to give up when it comes to privacy. The other one, Weekly Thing Studio, was interesting because that was an example where I felt like I could use AI coding tools and maybe create anything. So I didn't really know what I wanted. I kind of framed it up as this idea that if I had a creative studio filled with agents that could work on projects alongside me, that I think that could be really powerful. Ultimately, it never worked. I tried having a number of different agents working, having them collaborate. I never was able to get that to produce anything of value, and I ended up abandoning it very quickly, because it just always became overly complicated. It's interesting because you could say that the studio is very similar to what Elixir Bot originally started off as, whereas Elixir Bot was an agent to run the whole clan, and then I eventually flipped that completely inside out, building Elixir as software and databases and all this with an agentic layer on top. The studio is very similar in that I tried to build an agent solution for everything, and ultimately realized that WT Builder and the weekly website, those were the better solutions, and then build a thin agentic layer on top, which WT Builder has.

### 28. Open Loop, Charlie Brown Tree, Stone/Steel/People, Jiggy Thing Puzzles, Yearly Thing 2025 are archived, complete as they are. What makes something finished rather than retired?

**Jamie:** Well, these projects all came to a natural conclusion. Open Loop was a podcast that Garrick and I did, and we decided that it was time for that to come to a landing. The Charlie Brown Tree was a very finite project. We had that carved out of a dead tree at our cabin, and it's an amazing piece of art. Jiggy Thing Puzzles was a funny one because we realized how hard it is to make a jigsaw puzzle. It took a tremendous amount of effort for us to design the jigsaw puzzle. And then the cost of actually producing a jigsaw puzzle, you know, at subscale are just crazy. We realized that by trying to make puzzles, we were spending the same amount of money we were raising because if you buy in quantities of less than a thousand, the costs are just way too high. So all of them just had natural kind of landing points or it became obvious that they weren't worth pursuing.

### 29. The Weekly Thing GPT was replaced by Thingy. What did the GPT teach you about what a real agent needed to be?

**Jamie:** Yeah, it taught me two things. One, it taught me that just sending a bunch of data into that system both was not going to give me the kind of experience that I wanted, but it gave me enough of a taste that there was something here and that if I could build a real agent around it, that that could be meaningful. But the very basic ability to just kind of copy paste a ton of files or a ton of JSON into the GPT just wasn't robust enough for what I really wanted.

### 30. Which thing had the most people using it at its peak, and did that matter to you?

**Jamie:** You know, probably the Foreground MediaWiki skin, really. I mean, because that is adopted by Wikimedia, and there are a lot of websites and MediaWiki installs that run that. And I know for sure it has the most GitHub stars of any project that I host. I mean, by a long ways. And generally, no, that doesn't mean a lot to me. I would say that I really have come to appreciate... so let me edit that response though, because right now there are over 1,600 people who subscribe to the Weekly Thing, who read it, who engage with it on a regular basis. So I feel like now by far the largest audience, if you will, that I have is the one that reads the Weekly Thing. And that's the thing that the most people engage with, you know, by a long ways. And then in terms of actual software projects or other things, yeah, I think Weekly Thing is by far the number one.
