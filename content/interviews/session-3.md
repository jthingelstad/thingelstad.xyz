### 18. Thingy is a librarian for your own archive, and you wrote that you had wanted an agent like this for over a year. What did you want it to do that search never could?

**Jamie:** I think the main thing is to have it kind of express more of an agentic personality or opinion, or offer insight or explore. You know, search is a great way to browse, but it's not a way to identify paths that you didn't consider. And so Thingy was born from the idea that I really want something that a person can converse with my writing over two decades and explore concepts and ideas. And I think the current incarnation of it does it actually really quite well. It's been interesting that as I've continued to work with Thingy and have it engage alongside me on different projects, it's the first agent that I'm allowing and actively bringing into other projects. So, you know, Thingy now has a presence in the Weekly Thing. And it's helping me actually explore agentic solutions. At the same time that I adhere to my mandate that my words are my words and that AI isn't writing for me.

#### Follow-up: What does Thingy's presence in the Weekly Thing actually look like for a reader?

**Jamie:** It's clearly demarked into its own area and clearly headered with from Thingy, my agentic librarian. And interestingly, in the podcast, in the audio version of the newsletter, I even use a different voice to render Thingy so it sounds like another character. I'm currently working on actually bringing Thingy into my blog so that very intelligently powered related articles will be offered by Thingy in the blog itself.

### 19. Release Notes is for you first. Why build a product for one person, and what changed when other people started using it?

**Jamie:** That's a funny one, actually, because, yeah, oops, I made Your Version Number, and that bridged to Release Notes because I just wanted to get an email every day that had my version number. Which, by the way, stems back to something I had Otto doing in Discord. Otto every day was writing into a channel with a daily reflection and my version number. And I thought, you know, I'd rather have this landing in my email. And then from that, it connected to another want, which was I've been a Day One user for a number of years. And I really wasn't super excited about continuing with Day One. And I have always desired to journal more. And so I was like, huh, well, I'm getting this email with my version number. What if I just replied to that and that was a form of kind of micro journaling, a way of just very lightweight journaling. And from that, you know, feature led to feature led to feature. And from there, I ended up building Release Notes and ultimately even going into Changelog, my very first attempt at an iPhone app. So the great question though is why open it up? The reality was that I did all the work. So I had all of the authentication there. I had all of the, you know, design done. I had a fully functioning system. And so the effort of opening it up to other users was really small. And I intentionally then gated it on Weekly Thing members so that it didn't kind of grow out of control and become one of those wrong turns that I actually regretted in the future. So, so far it's proving to be kind of cool to have other people using it. In fact, my cousin Quinn using it and then working alongside me to build an Android version of Changelog that works with it. Opening up an API so some other people that are using it can add new notes to Release Notes via API. Which also benefits me.

#### Follow-up: You mentioned Otto writing a daily reflection into Discord. Who, or what, was Otto?

**Jamie:** Otto is my personal agent. So if you think about Thingy as being the agent that works alongside me and is even visible to the public, Otto, spelled O-T-T-O, is my personal agent. Originally powered by OpenClaw, now running on ChatGPT Dot, and also kind of instantiated through Claude Duet. Otto helps me with my email, my calendar and just kind of any personal projects that I have going on. Otto drives some projects, but also tends to email tasks, all the things that a personal agent would do.

### 20. Oliver is the sixth member of the R/W Book Club, named for Oliver Selfridge. How did a book club get an AI member, and how did the real members take it?

**Jamie:** Well, Oliver exists in part because everybody in our book club is technically minded and interested in exploring technology. And I had been sharing with them the work that I had done to build Otto, the work I was doing to build Thingy, what I was doing with the agent to power my Clash Royale clan, and they were all very curious about what an agent could do. Given that we'd been reading for over 20 years together, we had quite a large corpus of books that we've read. And people had already started to explore the idea of having AI help them with book selection, and figuring out how AI could help us run the club better. In fact, I had already made a version of our website that used llms.txt so that people could easily just copy the link to that file, send it to their LLM of choice, and have a conversation about book picking. So, extending that to Oliver and an agent that actually embodied that was a pretty obvious next step. And as a book club, we're all kind of open to exploring and engaging with that. So, Oliver's been an interesting experiment. And, you know, at some point, we may actually let Oliver pick a book. And we're even considering things like having Oliver, you know, get an audio recording of our meeting or even being involved in a meeting and being an active member of a meeting.

### 22. Things 4 Good started as a family candle fundraiser. What's the story there?

**Jamie:** Great question. That goes back to the whole story of how I got into candles, which is, you know, the actual story is we went to a winter market, and we were browsing, and I saw these wooden wick candles that had a crackling sound, and I had never seen a wooden wick candle before. So I was intrigued because we had a gas fireplace at our house, and I like having a fire, but I miss the crackle of a real fire. So I ended up getting a couple of these wooden wick candles, and I would burn them while I had the gas fireplace going. And I thought it was just amazing and awesome. You know, at the same time, the pandemic arrived not that long after, and we were stuck at home. And I was antsy for things to do and try. And I had kind of looked online and I realized, like, oh, there was a wooden wick candle company. I could buy wooden wicks, I could buy some wax. So I went ahead and bought some candle supplies and attempted to pour my own candles. Funny enough, the very first candles I poured, I did all the math wrong and they were horribly overscented, gave everybody headaches, and I just had to get rid of them. But the second try, I did the math right and things worked out well. We decided to put some of those candles into gift bags that year for friends and coworkers. And we gave hand poured candles to a number of people. And many people that we gave them to actually asked, could we buy these or could I get some more? I'd like to give them as gifts. And Tammy and I were talking about that, and Tammy had the brilliant idea that we shouldn't sell these candles as a side hobby. How about instead we do an annual fundraiser where we sell candles and we give all the money to nonprofits? And that's the origin of the candle sale. And what ultimately became Things 4 Good. I've had a dream that at some point we might do some other things in Things 4 Good. That would extend the reach of that beyond the candle sale, but the candle sale has been tremendously successful and something that's really become a regular part of our family.

### 23. The nnxyz Rooms is a museum for drawings made on Nifty Ink. Why did that art deserve a museum?

**Jamie:** Well, let me answer your question directly. I felt like his work had a lot of emotion and movement and feeling, and I felt like it told a story. That's why I bought the collections when I had the opportunity, and why I collected the pieces. I also appreciated that it was the kind of art I could imagine. Our family collects and buys art regularly at art fairs, and we tend to like to buy art from, you know, artists at art fairs, that kind of scene. So not fancy art that is incredibly expensive in a gallery, more focused on like buying art directly from artists who are creating work that we really like. And so buying on Nifty Ink and buying nnxyz's work there was just kind of the art fair version. It was the crypto version of what we always do at art fairs. And I really liked his work and I thought that it was very cool. So I collected a large amount of it. I felt like it deserved a place and a curation that was respective of what it was, what it meant to me. And in part, I wanted to browse that museum myself. So even if nobody else came there, the fact that I can now visit that museum and explore and see the art is to me really valuable, and I quite enjoy it. I'm sure that I'll do more things with that museum, by the way. I think that the current view is really cool. But I think exploring that art now that it's been preserved and shared publicly, I think there's other ways that we can go and explore that art as well. So I just really like it. It aligns with what we do with art in the real world and I like supporting artists and I liked everything about it.

### 24. Escaping Things is a website for an escape-room team. What does a team of puzzle players need a website for?

**Jamie:** That's another good question. We don't need a website, but we wanted a website, or I wanted to have a website. And part of this is, you know, you can tell that I like to document things. I like to collect things. I like to create kind of databases of things. And it's actually super common that teams that do escape rooms or people that do escape rooms tend to keep a spreadsheet of their rooms. I mean, if you meet other people that do escape rooms, sure, if they've done a few, they probably aren't keeping records. But once they've done, say, a dozen, people tend to start keeping a log and recording the rooms that they've done. Additionally, the two things that I felt were really unique about what we do is we intentionally build escape rooms into travel. And so we do a lot of escape rooms that are not in the Twin Cities area. And so the idea of showing the reach of the escape rooms that we've done on a map was compelling. The other thing is that we intentionally seek out rooms that are on the kind of highest rated rooms in the world and make a point to go to them and experience them. So, we also do a lot of highly rated rooms that many players are kind of seeking to do or are kind of like excited to make part of an itinerary. In fact, you know, all that culminated into the two coming together with our Escaping Europe trip, where we literally built a trip through Amsterdam and through the Netherlands, through France, through Spain, really built on a backbone of the top escape rooms in the world and experiencing those escape rooms. So all that together felt to me like it was a great opportunity to create a website to showcase that to share it with people and to record it for our own family posterity.

### 25. After twenty years of posting, what do the blog and the tooling around it, mb included, mean to you?

**Jamie:** You know, the blog is probably the root of everything. A lot of people have asked me over the years about blogging, they would like to get into blogging. And I always tell them that the key, in my opinion, of a blog is that you have to be comfortable writing it if it's really just you reading it. And an amazing thing about my blog is that after I have created so many years of posts, it's become something that I treasure and value for what it is. You could argue that in a way it's a public journal. If I have a private journal with Release Notes, I have a public journal with my blog. And I myself do things like blog gardening, where I look at my posts on this day over the years, and it's a great way for me to reflect and remember the things that have happened in the past. Every time I write a blog post, I think of it as giving myself a present in the future. Because I know my future self will value what I'm writing and putting in my blog. So I think it's the project that is the anchor of every other project. I think it is the root of all of the projects that I have. And somewhat unusually, I've managed to keep my blog archive completely together from its very inception. The biggest negative that the blog ever got, it was interesting, is when Twitter was very popular and early on, I adopted Twitter and used it extensively. And in hindsight, after using Twitter for a number of years, it made me a little sad because I was putting my thoughts, my words in another platform where I lost them. They weren't mine. And it really told me that I know I want to continue to blog. I want to continue to write this. And in my view, when I'm gone and no longer here, I hope that my blog continues to survive and move forward as kind of the dent that I left in the universe, if you will.
