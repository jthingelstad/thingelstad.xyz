### 9. Road Sign Math began on February 13, 2005. Tell me about that first sign, and what made you think it was a game.

**Jamie:** That's funny. So I had driven I-94 many, many times. Growing up in North Dakota, I would drive I-94 from Minneapolis to Minot. And Anytime I was coming back on I-94 East, I would see this sign. And it was always this sign that said 33399. And to me, it was just obvious that you should go up to the sign, put a times sign and an equal sign, because here's this sign that was essentially a math equation just sitting right there. And I saw that sign dozens of times and thought, like, that's so funny. And then I thought, I wonder how many other signs there are like that. Like, how many other signs have that kind of math equation in them? And then I thought, well, I should go look for some. And then I found some. It was not easy to find them. They're not that common. But I had a lot of fun doing it. And then I think I was talking to friends. I don't even recall, but somehow the idea of making that into a website became a thing. And then I thought, oh, I wonder if other people would find this interesting. And that's when I created a wiki out of it, and we got other people going on it. But it was that original sign that to me it just seemed obvious that it was math. And I thought, huh, I wonder how many other signs there are like this.

### 11. Your links have had three homes: links_thing in 2012, the Link Blog, and now Linking Things, with every link since 2005. Why do saved links matter so much to you?

**Jamie:** Oh boy, this is a big one. I would just say I love the open web. The web was born when I was in my 20s in college, and just the delight of exploring and experiencing the web has always been something I just truly, really enjoy. I still remember the very first version of Mosaic that I ever used. It was unique in that every web server that existed in the world was pre-populated in the bookmarks. That's how early I was that I was able to see the web. So bookmarking on the web has just been something I've done forever. And I've kept that database for many, many years. That database is the thing that actually became the genesis for the Weekly Thing. I was interested in a way to publish the bookmarks that I had found interesting that week, and that was really the backbone for the Weekly Thing. Having that published as a database has always been a wish of mine. So I tried various versions. And none have really landed and been the thing that I really envisioned they could be. I think the current version is what I really hope that it could be.

### 12. Your Version Number started as a blog post in February 2018 and became Release Notes and now the Changelog app. What was the idea in that first post, and when did it turn into a product?

**Jamie:** Oh, the idea came from a conversation I was having with my friend David Hussman, and he and I were talking about this idea of software versions and talking about like, you know, having a major version, a minor version, and every day. And we were just riffing on this idea and really both just jamming on it being a really fun concept. And hence the blog came. I made it into a shortcut. I've done a lot with it over the years, but the thing that I always wanted was I wanted something to bring it into my daily activity. So I've had a shortcut for years that is showing it for me. I think there's a similar concept, by the way, with finitude and the 4,000 weeks concept. And so those things, the pace of time and the finitude of life, all connect together. And to me, Your Version Number is a really great way to bring that into daily life in a way that is just fun and interesting. And then attaching release notes to it just seemed like an obvious thing. It extends the metaphor. The software metaphor just extends naturally. And it makes total sense that you would attach the things that happen, the release notes of every day, to Your Version Number.

### 13. The Weekly Thing has been made three ways: by hand with Shortcuts, in the Workshop, and now in WT Builder. What did each get right, and what finally made you build an editor of your own?

**Jamie:** Well, to be clear, the workshop never actually generated an issue of the Weekly Thing. So I would say that the Weekly Thing has had four eras. The first being when it was originally launched on TinyLetter, the second being when it was running on MailChimp, the third being when it was running on Buttondown, and the fourth being still running on Buttondown, but now with WT Builder and me taking over the archive and hosting that myself. This fourth one is really filled with a lot of stuff because it's the WT Builder, it's me taking over the archive, it's creating a generated podcast that goes with it, there's a huge expansion that happened in this fourth one. But the first three are really all about me learning the content cycle and figuring out the right platform for sending the email out. TinyLetter was great, it was aligned well to my indie web ethos and kind of thinking about hosting it in a certain way. But it lacked core functionality. Most obviously, I could not schedule the email to send at a time in the future. So I typically wanted it to be sent on Saturdays at 7 a.m. TinyLetter couldn't do that. So TinyLetter just didn't have the capability, and I had no API to send it in. So I moved to MailChimp pretty quickly, I think after about a year. Because MailChimp had way more powerful capability. I could automate sending the newsletter to it with the API. I could schedule it. But in fact, MailChimp was kind of a poor fit because it was really heavy on HTML. It really is a marketing tool, not a newsletter tool. And once my content became HTML, I really couldn't edit it. I didn't like that in MailChimp, I was sending a campaign. I'm not sending a campaign, and I have a strong aversion to advertising and all these things. So I really never felt at home in MailChimp. When Buttondown came on the scene, Buttondown seemed like an obvious and amazing platform. It was designed for newsletters. It was designed to allow individuals to send. I loved that it used Markdown, because then my authoring environment, my automation environment, could be all Markdown. And so I created this kind of complex sequence of shortcuts that would generate the Markdown, which all worked really well. However, the biggest issue was it was a one-way pipe. So I could create content, but if I changed anything further down the line, it was really difficult to make that change back in the source material. And the other thing was it was not at all what you see is what you get. So I would build it in an entirely different environment than what it actually got created as and read as, which meant that I could end up creating something that kind of didn't look right. And the last thing I'd say is it was very rigid. I couldn't dynamically change stuff, not easily, on a single individual issue. So all that stuff is why I ended up creating WT Builder, again enabled by the fact that I could build it with agentic technology, because otherwise it would have been just incredibly expensive and far too time consuming to create an application that only I would ever use.

### 14. Elixir began as a Clash Royale data project and became an MCP server, a collector, a Discord bot, and a clan. How did a game turn into that much software?

**Jamie:** Well, it actually begins further back than you're thinking because it wasn't just a data project. Elixir started as a Discord bot and the intention was this was me learning about agents. And so what I wanted to figure out is could I create an agent that would help me run and manage a Clash Royale clan? That was the question. The premise was could an agent help me run and manage a Clash Royale clan? I realized that Clash Royale has an API that allows you to get read-only access to what's happening in the game. That was a key unlock. And then I realized like Discord would be an interesting control surface where the clan could congregate. So that's where it all started, with a Discord bot written in Python using the Clash Royale API to help me and my fellow clan leaders run the clan. What you see in the data project that became Elixir is essentially retrospectively looking at everything we built in the bot and realizing that we could decompose it into individual capabilities that each operated better and kind of reverse the whole stack, flip it inside out into a data platform that is then building product capability and then enabling LLM agent capability on top of that.

### 15. POAP: the Airport Rally, the 612 Challenge, POAP2RSS, and now Proof of a Person after the protocol wound down. What did you see in POAP, and what survived its ending?

**Jamie:** I loved POAPs for a couple of different reasons. One, I wanted to find ways to explore and use crypto technology to create things. And one of the things I loved about POAPs is that they were a completely non-economic use case of crypto. I found it challenging that almost everything that happened inside of the crypto ecosystem was fundamentally about putting a price on something and buying and selling it. And what I loved about POAPs is that there was no price, there was no buying and selling, there was no economic activity at all. It was simply a protocol that served a purpose and it also served something very interesting because it used blockchain in an interesting way. It allowed me to assert that I did a thing without relying on any company or other kind of independent arbiter to be able to host and own that data. Instead, that data lived on the blockchain and really delivered that promise of blockchain technology. POAP informed my thinking about crypto in so many powerful ways. I became known by the POAP ecosystem. The founder of POAP and I have exchanged emails. I created many POAPs. I onboarded people to crypto using POAPs. It just became something that I truly, really cherished. I think also I've always had a collector mentality. As a kid, I loved collecting things. I still remember when I collected every single M\*A\*S\*H figurine, for example. And POAPs were a way of collecting. And so the 612 POAP Challenge was kind of the culmination of that, if you will. The idea of creating a game that ran on the POAP ecosystem that I could pull people into and play in the real world. The reality is, POAP was kind of doomed, I think, in part because crypto has never answered the onboarding problem. And additionally, the reputation of crypto became very sullied with all of the fraud and theft that occurs there. And so I think it's very difficult for any idea, no matter how good it is, to escape the shadow of the crypto industry right now.

### 16. WikiApiary, the Planet Kubb wiki, Wikinosh. There was a MediaWiki era. What drew you to wikis, and why did it end?

**Jamie:** Very simply, Semantic MediaWiki. I loved what you could build with Semantic MediaWiki by bringing data and text together in a way that allowed you to kind of play with surfaces like they were clay. And all of those ideas, all of those sites fundamentally were built out of Semantic MediaWiki. You could connect the dots, by the way, back to AI and that I'm just not a very good coder. Those are all things that you could build with software, but I really wasn't able to build them with software. And building them with software would have meant that there was one or two people that had to do everything. And the idea of Semantic MediaWiki and being able to create these surfaces dynamically at runtime and iterate on them very fast was just like super fun. Ultimately, the MediaWiki ecosystem is not powerful enough to run these kinds of applications. It was too janky. Like, if you think about how WikiApiary worked, I mean, hack is the best term that you could apply to it. But I still think that those concepts are super fun and like a great, enjoyable thing to build. You know, the semantic web and semantic media was a great idea. It ended up never really flourishing the way that ultimately could have happened. I think microformats today are probably the biggest real thing that we still have on the web that's a result from that era. And that's what ultimately made me walk away from it was that you could create these solutions, but they all kind of fell under their own weight because of the complexities and challenges of MediaWiki underneath.
